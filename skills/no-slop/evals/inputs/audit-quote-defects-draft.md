# Why Developer Tools Live or Die by Positioning

In today's rapidly evolving developer-tools landscape, positioning isn't just a nice-to-have, but a survival trait. Positioning — the way a tool claims a category before anyone asks — decides which products become defaults and which become line items in a churn report.

## The default, not the sale

Mara Voss puts it best: "Developers don't buy tools. They join defaults." It's important to note that this changes the entire go-to-market motion. You are not persuading a buyer; you are colonizing a habit. The winning tools get adopted sideways — one engineer, one repo, one CI pipeline at a time — until procurement is a formality.

In short, the sale is downstream of the default.

## The first tweet is the category

Voss again: "Your category is decided in your first tweet, not in the launch post." Most teams spend six weeks polishing a launch page and six minutes on the sentence that actually names the thing. By the time the launch post ships, developers have already filed you next to whatever you first resembled.

This matters because of Voss's concept of crossing the chasm — the gap between early adopters and the mainstream that swallows most tools whole.

In short, you don't pick your category; your first public sentence does.

## Comparison is surrender

As Voss warns: "If you need a comparison table, you have already lost the category." A comparison table concedes that you live on someone else's axis. The strongest developer brands — Stripe in payments, Vercel in deployment — never argued they were better. They argued they were different, and then let the defaults compound.

In short, don't compare; rename the game.

Ultimately, positioning is a compounding asset. By understanding these dynamics, founders can leverage their earliest public language to supercharge adoption before the market files them somewhere they'll never escape.
